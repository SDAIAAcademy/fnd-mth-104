Which correction, and what does it promise?

A safety report makes five claims, each of which would trigger a costly recall. The analyst wants a low chance of even one false claim. Which procedure fits?

Benjamini-Hochberg
No correction, α = 0.05 each
Holm, which controls the family-wise error rate
Report only the claim with the smallest p-value
Why. Each false claim is costly, so control the chance of any: Holm does, with more power than Bonferroni.

Checkpoint

What does the null result say?

A pilot with 10 customers per group finds no significant effect of a new menu on spending. A simulation says the test had 18% power for the effect that would matter. What should the report say?

The new menu does not change spending
The false-finding rate was 82%
The new menu reduces spending
The pilot was too small to tell; it would miss that effect about four times in five
Why. With 18% power a real effect usually goes undetected; report the wide interval, not "no effect".

Block B · findings

With few real effects and low power, nearly half the findings are false

100 ideas tested, 10 realExpected
False findings: 90 × 0.054.5
True findings: 10 × 0.5 power5.0
Share of findings that are false47%

Not 5%

α is the rate among true nulls, not the share of findings that are wrong. That share depends on power and on how many ideas are real.

From slide_numbers.py, week 8. This is FND-MTH-103's base-rate reasoning applied to findings. A frequent LO2 misreading: "p < 0.05, so there is a 5% chance this finding is false".

Block B · practical significance

Compare the interval with the smallest effect that would change the decision

Worth it

The whole interval is above the threshold.

Not worth it

The whole interval is below it, even if p is tiny.

Unclear

The interval straddles it: more data, or a decision that accepts the risk.

The threshold comes from the business, before the analysis: the cost of the change divided by what each unit of effect is worth. The lab uses the finance analyst's 1.50 riyals a basket.

Checkpoint

Decide

A new delivery route must save at least 3 minutes a trip to justify the change. A trial gives a saving of 1.1 minutes, 95% interval 0.6 to 1.6, p < 0.001. What is the decision?

Adopt it: the saving is highly significant
Do not adopt it: even the top of the interval is below 3 minutes
Unclear: run a larger trial
Adopt it: p below 0.001 means a large saving
Why. The effect is real and too small to matter: the interval sits below the threshold.

Block C · honest reporting

Every result travels with its scope, its limits and how it was checked

Part of the noteWhat it says
ResultThe estimate, its interval, the test, in plain words
Checked byA hand case, a second method, a rerun with a new seed, a known truth
LimitsScope, confounding, the number of tests, suppressed groups
ReviewerA second person reruns it before it is used

The four-part note of every lab is the capstone's standard. The reviewer line is segregation of duties: the analyst who ran it does not sign it off alone.

A two-page brief on a public dataset: estimate, interval, test, effect size and limits

  • A public dataset whose licence allows reuse, with no personal data
  • One question, written down with its analysis before you look
  • The notebook reruns; intervals agree with a new seed

Three minutes on the brief; seven rerunning one test with a change the assessor specifies, and reading the result.

Checkpoint

What is missing from this note?

A note says: "Result: the new rota cut overtime by 6 hours a month (interval 2 to 10). Checked by: an assistant wrote the code. Limits: one region. Reviewer: the team lead." What is the weakest part?

"Checked by": it says who wrote the code, not how the result was verified
"Result": it should give the p-value instead of the interval
"Limits": a limit should never be stated in a report
"Reviewer": the analyst should review their own work
Why. A check is something run: a hand case, a second method, a rerun. An assistant writing the code is not a check.

Answer A.
