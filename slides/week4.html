Week 2 measured the standard error by drawing new samples from a known population. In real work there is one sample and no population. This week's trick: let the sample stand in for the population, and resample it.

Review · week 3

Why does the overall rate mislead?

Branches that chose a new oven burn more trays overall, but fewer within both small and large branches. Large branches chose the oven more often and burn more trays with any oven. What is branch size here?

The outcome of the comparison
A source of random error that averages out
The treatment
A confounder: it drives both the choice and the burn rate
Why. Size affects who got the oven and how many trays burn, so the overall comparison mixes the two.

Block A · the idea

Treat the sample as the population, and draw new samples from it

1 · Resamplen values from the sample, with replacement
→
2 · ComputeThe statistic
→
3 · RepeatThousands of times
→
4 · ReadThe spread of the statistics
With replacement, because without it every resample would be the sample itself, reordered.

Compare with week 2's flow: the same four steps, with the sample in the place of the population. The standard deviation of the bootstrap statistics estimates the standard error.

Block A · the interval

A 95% percentile interval keeps the middle 95% of the bootstrap statistics

n = len(x)
# B resamples at once
idx = rng.integers(0, n, size=(B, n))
boots = x[idx].mean(axis=1)
low, high = np.percentile(boots, [2.5, 97.5])
  • Works for any statistic: swap np.mean for np.median
  • B of 2,000 or more keeps the ends steady; report B and the seed

Explain the indexing trick: one B × n matrix of random positions, then one vectorised statistic per row. Participants write this function in the lab and reuse it in week 5.

Block B · meaning

The 95% belongs to the method, not to one interval

  • Draw many samples; build an interval from each
  • A 95% method contains the true value in about 95% of them
  • Any one interval either contains it or does not; we do not know which

A correct sentence

"Our method gives intervals that contain the true mean in about 95% of samples; from this sample the interval is A to B."

The LO2 sheet in the second test has ten statements to classify and correct; most misreadings come from forgetting that the 95% is about the method.

Block B · misreadings

Four common misreadings of a confidence interval

MisreadingCorrection
"95% of the values lie in the interval"The interval is for the mean (or median), not for single values
"The true mean has a 95% chance of being in this interval"The 95% describes how often the method works
"A wider interval means a worse method"Width reflects the data and n; a too-narrow method under-covers
"Two overlapping intervals mean no difference"Overlap does not settle it: build an interval for the difference (week 5)

Ask participants to say which misreading they have seen in a report. The overlap rule is wrong in both directions and is common in dashboards.

Block C · libraries

Check which interval a library returns: SciPy's default is not the percentile method

from scipy import stats
# what you build in the lab
stats.bootstrap((x,), np.mean,
                method="percentile")
# the default method: BCa
stats.bootstrap((x,), np.mean)
BCa (bias-corrected and accelerated) adjusts the ends; why it does is in SPC-STA-201. Name the method in every report.

Sources: SciPy documentation of scipy.stats.bootstrap, which gives method 'BCa' as the default. In R the infer package and the boot package have their own defaults; the habit is the same: read the documentation of the version you use.

Checkpoint

What does this coverage study show?

A coverage study of a 95% interval method on a long-tailed population gives 0.86 at n = 12 and 0.94 at n = 150, from 1,000 samples each. What is the right conclusion?

The method is close to its 95% at n = 150 and too narrow at n = 12
The method fails at both sizes, since neither shows exactly 0.95
Use more bootstrap resamples at n = 12 and it will reach 0.95
The intervals at n = 150 are more often wrong because they are narrower
Why. 0.94 is within simulation error of 0.95; 0.86 is far below it.

Answer A. The Monte Carlo error of a coverage estimate from 1,000 samples is about ±0.014 (two standard errors).
